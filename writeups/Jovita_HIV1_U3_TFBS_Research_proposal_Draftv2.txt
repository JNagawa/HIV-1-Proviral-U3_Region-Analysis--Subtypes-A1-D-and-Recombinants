Association of U3 Transcription Factor Binding Site Abundance and Genetic Architecture with 
HIV-1 Latent Viral Reservoir Reactivation in subtypes A1, D and Recombinants. 
 
ABSTRACT 
Background: Human immunodeficiency virus type 1 (HIV -1) persists during antiretroviral therapy 
(ART) through establishment of transcriptionally silent but replication-competent proviruses which is the 
major barrier to HIV -1 cure (Churchill et al., 2016; Siliciano & Si liciano, 2022). Strategies aimed at 
reversing HIV latency seek to induce viral transcription and expression (Maina et al., 2021) however, 
clinical and ex vivo studies have demonstrated that only a fraction of intact proviruses respond to 
stimulation (Ho et al., 2013, Siliciano & Siliciano, 2021, Khanal et al., 2021). HIV reservoir reactivation 
is driven by host transcription factors binding to cis-regulatory motifs concentrated within the U3 region 
of the HIV-1 long terminal repeat (LTR), however, these binding sites remain poorly characterized across 
HIV-1 subtypes A1, D, and recombinant forms prevalent in Uganda and East Africa  (Mbondji-Wonje et 
al., 2018). 
Objectives: This study aims to: (1) systematically locate, quantify, and compare TFBS abundance and 
sequence architecture for key TFs (NF-κB, SP1, NFAT, AP-1, TBP, and TFIID) across subtypes A1, D, 
and recombinants using intact near -full-length proviral sequences; (2)  quantify predicted binding score 
variations attributable to single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and copy number differences; and (3) 
correlate U3 TFBS profiles with empirical basal transcription and post -stimulation LVR reactivation 
outcomes. 
Methodology: A cross -sectional analytical study will be conducted using near-full-length proviral 
sequences generated from Full-Length Intact Proviral Sequencing (FLIP -SEQ) and the HIV Single 
Molecule Real-Time Capture Assay (HIV SMRTcap) and metadata of the Rakai HIV Reservoir Cohort 
study. U3 region T ranscription Factor Binding Sites (TFBS) will be computationally mapped using 
JASPAR position weight matrices (PWMs) and the FIMO tool. Motif profiles will be correlated with 
matched HIV-1 transcription profiling data and Quantitative Viral Outgrowth Assay (QVOA) reactivation 
metrics using gener alised linear and mixed -effects regression models, adjusting for duration on ART, 
CD4⁺ T-cell count, and viral subtype.

Introduction 
The HIV latent viral reservoir (LVR) refers to long -lived resting CD4⁺ T cells that harbour 
transcriptionally silent but replication -competent integrated HIV -1 proviruses. These cells are 
immunologically indistinguishable from uninfected cells, rendering t hem invisible to the host immune 
system and insensitive to Combination Antiretroviral Therapy (cART), which exclusively targets active 
viral replication (Khanal et al., 2021; Siliciano & Siliciano, 2022). Latency reversal is defined as the 
pharmacological or immunological induction of viral transcription and protein production from these 
inactive proviruses. The long terminal repeat (LTR) serves as the predominant regulatory element driving 
viral transcription and is structured into three sub -regions: U3 (unique 3' segment, containing regulatory 
elements), R (repeated region, including the trans -activation response element, TAR), and U5 (unique 5' 
segment). The U3 sub -region is further subdivided into the modulatory region ( –454 to –104 nt), core 
enhancer (–105 to –79 nt), and core promoter ( –78 to –1 nt), each harbouring binding sites for specific 
host transcription factors (Pereira et al., 2000; Delannoy et al., 2019). 
 
HIV-1 remains one of the foremost public health epidemics, with 39.9 million people living with the virus 
globally in 2023 (UNAIDS, 2023). Sub -Saharan Africa accounts for approximately 53% of all people 
living with HIV despite comprising roughly 10% of the global population (Horvath, 2024). Uganda bears 
a signif icant share of this burden and is characterised by an HIV epidemic predominantly driven by 
subtypes A1, D, and circulating or unique recombinant forms (CRFs/URFs) rather than the globally 
common subtype C or the well -studied subtype B (Prodger et al., 2017; Kankaka et al., 2023). Despite 
decades of cART, HIV-1 infection remains incurable because the LVR persists for the lifetime of the host; 
modelling estimates indicate that even with effective ART -mediated suppression, clearance of the 
reservoir would require more than 70 years (Siliciano & Siliciano, 2022; McMyn et al., 2023). 
 
Viral transcription is the proximal determinant of latency and reactivation. Under resting conditions 
(latency), the HIV-1 LTR is kept transcriptionally silent through limited nuclear availability of activating 
transcription factors, repressive chromatin organisation, and epigenetic silencing (Delannoy et al., 2019; 
Rodari et al., 2021). Upon host cell stimulation via T-cell receptor (TCR) signalling or cytokine exposure, 
downstream kinase cascades drive nuclear translocation of key host TFs most notably NF-κB, NFAT, and 
AP-1 which bind cooperatively to U3 cis -regulatory elements, recruit RNA polymerase II, and initiate 
high-velocity viral transcription (Pereira et al., 2000; Burnett et al., 2010). The basal transcription

machinery components TBP and TFIID anchor the pre -initiation complex at the TATA box within the 
core promoter, while three SP1 binding sites in the core promoter sustain basal activity and cooperate with 
NF-κB in enhancer-driven activation (Verma et al., 2016; Pal et al., 2022). 
 
The U3 region exhibits substantial inter -clade nucleotide divergence, reaching up to 25% across HIV -1 
subtypes (Mbondji-Wonje et al., 2018). Such variation is not biologically neutral: the duplication of the 
NF-κB binding site in subtype C viruses confers markedly enhanced transcriptional output and a more 
stable latent state (Pal et al., 2022; Verma et al., 2016). Furthermore, published Massively Parallel 
Reporter Assay (MPRA) data demonstrate that natural LTR sequence variants spanning even a few 
nucleotides can produce large, reproducible differences in transcriptional velocity and promoter drive 
(Burnett et al., 2010).  Foundational studies working with subtype B reference sequences, engineered 
reporter constructs, and plasma -derived viral RNA have progressively characterised how specific U3 
TFBS arrangements govern transcriptional output across HIV -1 subtypes. Pereira et al. (2000) compiled 
a comprehensive catalogue of cellular TF interactions with the subtype B LTR, establishing NF-κB, SP1, 
NFAT, and AP-1 binding sites as the core drivers of HIV-1 promoter activity and providing the reference 
framework against which all subsequent subtype comparisons have been made. Building on this, Burnett 
et al. (2010) demonstrated using MPRA that subtypes A and D harb our likely compromised Sp1 site II 
and Sp1 site III respectively deviations predicted to reduce TF binding affinity and alter the transcriptional 
output of these clades relative to subtype B. Verma et al. (2016) further showed that in subtype C, the 
physical proximity and precise orientation between the subtype -specific NF -κB motif (C -κB) and the 
adjacent Sp1III site are indispensable for full promoter activation, with disruption of this spatial 
relationship severely abrogating gene expression even when all  other binding sites remain intact. 
Extending these observations, Pal et al. (2022) demonstrated that NF -κB copy number directly and 
proportionally governs transcriptional strength in subtype C, with higher NF -κB site counts accelerating 
both transcriptional activation and latency establishment, establishing a direct dose-response relationship 
between TFBS abundance and promoter drive. At the inter -clade level, Mbondji -Wonje et al. (2018) 
catalogued U3 sequence variation across 16 HIV -1 clades and recombinant forms using plasma -derived 
viral sequences, revealing that subtype D carries distinct C/EBP-like motifs and subtype A1 displays non-
canonical AP-1/CREB and reduced USF -1 E-box conservation patterns that diverge substantially from 
the subtype B reference  framework on which most mechanistic latency studies have been based. 
Collectively, these studies establish that U3 TFBS architecture is both subtype -specific and functionally 
consequential, yet their reliance on reference strains, engineered constructs, and plasma-derived sequences

leaves the TFBS landscape of intact proviruses within the latent reservoir of subtypes A1, D, and 
recombinants largely uncharacterised. 
 
The Rakai Health Sciences Program (RHSP) Latent Viral Reservoir (LVR) Cohort in Uganda has 
generated a unique paired dataset of near -full-length proviral sequences alongside empirical viral 
outgrowth data. Prodger et al. (2017) demonstrated that virally suppressed Ugandans carry a median LVR 
size of 0.36 IUPM approximately three -fold lower than an equivalent American cohort hinting that viral 
genetic and host factors unique to non-B subtypes meaningfully modulate reservoir biology. Despite this, 
only a few studies have systematically quantified TFBS abundance and motif sequence architecture across 
subtypes A1, D, and recombinants, nor correlated these U3  level molecular features with functional 
QVOA-based reactivation. This study introduces this precise integration, potentially revealing viral 
genetic predictors of reactivation responsiveness that are critically needed to optimise latency -reversing 
agent (LRA) strategies for African and globally diverse populations. 
 
Problem statement 
Despite recognition that host transcription factor (TF) binding to U3 motifs is the proximal driver of HIV-
1 reactivation (Churchill et al., 2016; Siliciano & Siliciano, 2022), these binding sites remain poorly 
characterized across HIV-1 subtypes A1, D, and recombinant forms prevalent in Uganda and East Africa. 
Latency reversal agents (LRAs) pursuing the "shock-and-kill" strategy have consistently underperformed 
in clinical trials, reactivating fewer than 5% of latent proviruses even in combination regimens , largely 
because the molecular determinants of proviral inducibility remain incompletely defined (Debrabander et 
al., 2023; Spivak & Planelles, 2018). 
Studies examining plasma -derived U3 sequences from subtypes A1, D, and recombinant forms have 
identified subtype-specific deviations in Sp1, C/EBP, and AP -1/CREB binding site architecture, notably 
compromised Sp1 site III and distinct NF -κB enhancer configurations in subtype D, and non -canonical 
C/EBP and reduced USF -1 E -box conservation in subtype A1  suggesting that these sequence -level 
differences carry functional consequences for transcriptional regulation (Burnett et al., 2010; Mbondji -
Wonje et al., 2018). However, these studies relied on plasma-derived viral RNA and consensus reference 
sequences, which represent circulating replicating virus rather than primary proviral sequences integrated 
within resting CD4⁺ T cells , the actual cellular compartment constituting the latent reservoir. Critically, 
while TFBS patterns have been catalogued in reference and plasma -derived sequences, a systematic

position weight matrix (PWM) -based quantification of TFBS abundance in intact proviruses from 
subtypes A1, D, and recombinants, linked to empirical QVOA reactivation outcomes from virally 
suppressed individuals, is minimally studied . Additionally, it remains unresolved whether TFBS 
abundance exerts a cumulative, dose -dependent effect on inducible transcription or whether a minimum 
threshold configuration suffices for reactivation. To close this gap, this study will systematically quantify 
and characterise U 3 TFBS abundance and sequence architecture in intact proviruses derived from the 
latent reservoir of virally suppressed participants in the Rakai HIV Reservoir Cohort, and directly correlate 
these profiles with matched transcription and QVOA reactivation data. 
 
Significance 
This study will generate a comprehensive, patient-derived TFBS abundance map for HIV-1 subtypes A1, 
D, and recombinants, filling a critical void in non-B HIV cure research. By linking U3 TFBS architecture 
to empirical QVOA-derived reactivation metrics from a well-characterised Ugandan cohort, findings will 
provide actionable mechanistic insights to predict and stratify LVR reactivation potential. Results will 
directly inform the rational design and optimisation of subtype -inclusive shock-and-kill and block -and-
lock therapeutic strategies. All bioinformatic pipelines developed will be deposited as open -source tools 
on GitHub, benefiting the global retrovirology community. 
 
Research Questions 
1. What are the quantitative differences in the frequency, spatial positioning, and sequence 
composition of key U3 TFBS (NF-κB, SP1, NFAT, AP-1, TBP, TFIID) among and within HIV-1 
subtypes A1, D, and their recombinants? 
2. To what extent do sequence variants and copy number differences at U3 TFBS alter predicted 
transcription factor binding scores across and within HIV-1 subtypes A1, D, and their 
recombinants? 
3. How do U3 TFBS abundance profiles and sequence configurations correlate with baseline viral 
transcription levels and post-stimulation QVOA reactivation kinetics?

Methodology 
Study Design and data acquisition 
This is a cross -sectional analytical study utili zing existing prospectively collected clinical, virological, 
and genomic data from the Rakai HIV Reservoir Cohort.  
Study Site and Setting 
The study will be conducted at African Center of Excellence in Bioinformatics and Intensive data 
sciences- Makerere University in collaboration with the Rakai Health Sciences Program (RHSP), Kalisizo, 
Rakai District, Uganda and University of Western Ontario, Canada.  
Study Population 
The source population is the RHSP Latent Viral Reservoir (LVR) Cohort, comprising 90 HIV-1-infected 
adults (≥18 years) who have maintained viral suppression on ART for at least one year (two plasma HIV-
1 RNA measurements of <40 copies/mL, 10 –18 months apar t, with no intervening detectable result), 
predominantly infected with HIV -1 subtypes A1, D, and recombinants (Prodger et al., 2020; Mumby et 
al., 2025). The target study population is a subset of cohort participants for whom paired near-full-length

proviral sequences (FLIP -SEQ or HIV SMRTcap), HIV -1 transcription profiling data, and QVOA

Sample Size 
The analytic sample will include all cohort participants ( ~40 individuals) for whom at least one intact 
proviral sequence, a matched HIV transcription profile, and a QVOA measurement are simultaneously 
available. This is estimated at 40–70 participants, providing sufficient statistical power (80%, α = 0.05) to 
detect a moderate correlation (r ≥ 0.35) between TFBS abundance and QVOA -derived reactivation 
outcomes using two-tailed Pearson/Spearman tests and adjusted regression. 
Sampling Technique 
A purposive (criterion-based) sampling strategy will be applied, selecting all eligible individuals from the 
RHSP LVR Cohort who simultaneously satisfy the inclusion criteria outlined below. No probability 
sampling will be applied, as this study uses a fully pre-existing dataset. 
 
Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria 
Inclusion criteria: (1) RHSP LVR Cohort participant aged ≥18 years; (2) HIV -1 positive and on 
suppressive ART with documented viral suppression (<40 copies/mL) for ≥1 year; (3) availability of ≥1 
intact or near -full-length HIV -1 proviral sequence (FLIP -SEQ or HIV SMRTcap) (4)  availability of 
matched basal HIV-1 transcription profiling data and/or QVOA outgrowth data; and (5) confirmed HIV -
1 subtype classification as A1, D, or CRF/URF. 
Exclusion criteria: (1) Participants with incomplete or failed U3 sequence assembly; (2) sequences 
classified as subtype B or C; (3) participants with documented ART treatment failure (viral load >400 
copies/mL) within 6 months before genomic sampling; and (4) proviral sequences with ambiguous clade 
classification precluding subtype assignment. 
 
Data Acquisition 
Near-full-length proviral sequence data (FASTQ files) and associated participant metadata, including 
QVOA reactivation outcomes and transcription profiling results, will be obtained from the Rakai Health 
Sciences Program (RHSP) Latent Viral Reservoir Cohor t. Access to these de -identified datasets will be 
granted through the established RHSP data request protocol, which requires submission of a study 
proposal for review and approval by the relevant data access committee prior to release.

Data Processing and Analysis 
Bioinformatic analysis will proceed in three sequential phases: 
Phase 1 - U3 Sequence Extraction and Subtyping: Full -length proviral sequences will be processed to 
isolate 5′ LTR U3 domains (nucleotides –454 to –1 relative to the transcription start site). Subtype 
classification will be validated using the Los Alamos Proviral Sequence Database COMET and automated 
phylogenetic alignment tools. 
Phase 2 -TFBS Mapping and Abundance Quantification: Extracted U3 sequences will be scanned using 
the FIMO tool (MEME Suite) with JASPAR 2024 PWMs for NF -κB (p50/p65), SP1, NFAT, AP -1 
(FOS/JUN), TBP, and TFIID subunits. Output metrics will include: TFBS count per se quence, genomic 
coordinates, binding affinity scores (log -likelihood ratio scores), and SNP -associated score deviations. 
Standardised, reproducible R /Python/Bash scripts will be developed for all analyses and deposited on 
GitHub. 
Phase 3 - Statistical Correlation and Regression: TFBS abundance profiles will be correlated with QVOA 
infectious units per million (IUPM) and baseline transcription levels using Spearman rank correlation. 
Multivariate generalised linear models (GLMs) and mixed -effects models will assess independent 
associations, adjusting for ART duration, baseline CD4⁺ T -cell count, pre-ART viral load set-point, sex, 
and HIV -1 subtype. Interaction terms will test whether subtype modifies the TFBS  reactivation 
association. 
Data Cleaning 
All sequence data will undergo standard quality control procedures: minimum sequence length thresholds, 
removal of hypermutated sequences (using the Los Alamos HYPERMUT tool), and exclusion of 
sequences with >5% ambiguous nucleotide calls in the U3 region.  Clinical metadata will be cross -
validated against RHSP primary records for completeness, and missing data managed using complete -
case analysis or multiple imputation where appropriate. 
Data Security 
All participant-linked data will be accessed only by authorised investigators through RHSP’s secure server 
infrastructure, consistent with RHSP data governance policies. Genomic sequences will be stored in de -
identified form, linked to participant records only through anonymised study IDs. Data transfer between 
RHSP (Uganda) , ACE -Uganda and Western University (Canada) will be encrypted (AES -256) in

Dissemination 
Findings will be disseminated through: (1) an MSc thesis submitted to Makerere University; (2) 
presentation at the Rakai Health Sciences Program scientific meeting, (3) at least one peer -reviewed 
manuscript in a high -impact virology or HIV cure journal; ( 4) presentation at HIV conferences; and (5)

Ethical considerations 
This study will use entirely pre-existing, previously consented biological samples and associated data from 
the RHSP LVR Cohort. Ethical approvals for the original cohort were obtained from the Institutional 
Review Boards (IRBs) of the Uganda Virus Research Institute (UVRI), the Uganda National Council for 
Science and Technology (UNCST), the National Institutes of Health (NIH, USA), and Western University 
(Canada) (Kankaka et al., 2023; Prodger et al., 2017). All original participants provided written informed 
consent at each study visit for future use of their de -identified data and samples in HIV-related research. 
An amendment/waiver for secondary use of existing data will be sought from the UVRI and UNCST IRBs 
prior to study commencement. Additional approval will be obtained from the Makerere University College 
of Health Sciences School of Biomedical Sciences Research Ethics Committee in fulfillment of the 
requirements for the conduct of a Master's thesis.  
No new biological samples will be collected, and there will be no direct participant contact, negating the 
need for re-consent. All data will be handled in a de-identified manner consistent with RHSP’s institutional 
data use policies. Privacy and confident iality will be maintained through strict access controls, 
anonymised study IDs, and encrypted data storage and transfer protocols . This study presents no greater 
than minimal risk to participants. Findings will be reported in aggregate without participant -identifiable 
information.
